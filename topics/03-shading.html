Lighting & Shading

Rasterization tells us which pixels a triangle covers. Shading decides what color each one gets. This chapter builds the classic lighting model piece by piece: normals, diffuse, specular, and where in the pipeline you evaluate them.

STEP 1Normals: which way does the surface face?

Every lighting formula in this chapter starts from one vector: the surface normal N, a unit-length arrow pointing straight out of the surface. Light that hits a surface head-on (along the normal) is brighter than light that grazes it, so the normal is how the shader knows the surface's orientation.

A triangle mesh is only an approximation of a smooth shape. A face normal is perpendicular to one flat triangle, computed with a cross product of two edges. Use it and every facet looks flat. A vertex normal is shared by all triangles that meet at a vertex, usually the normalized average of their face normals (or the exact normal of the original curved surface). Blending vertex normals across a triangle makes the faceted mesh look curved.

Positions transform with the model matrix M. Normals cannot, as soon as M contains a non-uniform scale. Stretch a circle 2× in x and a tangent direction stretches with it, but if you also stretch the normal it tilts toward the x axis and is no longer perpendicular. The fix is to transform normals with the inverse-transpose of the upper-left 3×3 of the matrix, then renormalize:

tangent: t' = M · t normal: n' = normalize( (M⁻¹)ᵀ · n ) check: n'ᵀ t' = nᵀ M⁻¹ M t = nᵀ t = 0 ✓

For pure rotations (and uniform scale) (M⁻¹)ᵀ equals M up to a scale factor, which is why the mistake often goes unnoticed. Old OpenGL called this matrix gl_NormalMatrix; today you compute it yourself and pass it to the shader.

Key idea. Normals are not positions. Interpolate vertex normals to fake smoothness, and transform them with (M⁻¹)ᵀ so they stay perpendicular under any scale.

STEP 2Lights and Lambert's cosine law

The classic model uses two kinds of light. A directional light is infinitely far away, like the sun: every point sees the same direction L and the same brightness. A point light sits at a position; L points from the surface toward it and changes across the surface, and its brightness falls off with distance d:

attenuation = 1 / (k_c + k_l·d + k_q·d²) here: k_c = 1, k_l = 0.1, k_q = 0.25 d = 2 → 1 / (1 + 0.2 + 1.0) = 0.45

A matte surface (paper, chalk, unpolished clay) scatters incoming light equally in all directions, so it looks equally bright from any viewpoint. How bright depends only on the angle θ between the normal and the light. Picture a beam of fixed width: when it arrives at an angle, its energy spreads over a footprint 1/cos θ times wider, so each patch of surface receives cos θ as much. That is Lambert's cosine law (Johann Lambert, 1760). Since N and L are unit vectors, cos θ is just a dot product:

diffuse = k_d · albedo · light_color · max(N·L, 0) θ = 0° → N·L = 1.00 θ = 60° → N·L = 0.50 θ = 90° → N·L = 0.00 θ > 90°: light is behind → clamp to 0

Notice the soft shading boundary on the sphere, called the terminator: it is where N·L crosses zero. Everything past it is black, because nothing in this model lets light bounce. That is the problem the ambient term in step 4 patches.

Key idea. Diffuse brightness is max(N·L, 0): one dot product per shading point, independent of where the camera is.

STEP 3Specular highlights: Phong vs Blinn-Phong

Shiny surfaces also show a bright highlight: a blurry reflection of the light. Unlike diffuse, it depends on where you look from, so we need the view vector V from the surface to the camera. In 1975 Bui Tuong Phong proposed reflecting L about the normal to get the mirror direction R, then measuring how close V is to it:

R = 2(N·L)N − L Phong: spec = max(R·V, 0)ⁿ Blinn-Phong: H = normalize(L + V) spec = max(N·H, 0)ⁿ

In 1977 Jim Blinn suggested the half vector H, halfway between L and V. When H lines up with N, the viewer is exactly in the mirror direction. N·H is cheaper when light and viewer are far away (H is then constant), it behaves better at grazing angles, and it was the form used by fixed-function OpenGL.

The exponent n is the shininess. Raising a number below 1 to a high power shrinks it quickly, so a big n gives a small, sharp highlight: with R·V = 0.95, n = 8 gives 0.66 but n = 128 gives 0.0014. In the plane, the angle between N and H is exactly half the angle between R and V, so Blinn-Phong's lobe is wider; you need roughly 4× the exponent to match Phong's highlight size.

Key idea. Specular depends on the viewer. Phong compares V with the mirror direction R; Blinn-Phong compares N with the half vector H. Shininess n controls how tight the highlight is.

STEP 4Putting the terms together

The full classic model adds three terms. The ambient term is a constant that stands in for all the light bouncing around the room, so shadowed sides are not pitch black. It is a crude hack (real indirect light is the subject of global illumination), but it is cheap.

color = k_a · albedo · C_light ambient + k_d · albedo · C_light · max(N·L, 0) diffuse + k_s · C_light · max(N·H, 0)ⁿ specular (per light, times attenuation for point lights; sum over lights)

Notice the specular term uses the light's color but not the albedo: on plastic the highlight is the color of the light, not the paint. Colors here are in linear light, where adding and multiplying is physically meaningful. The result is encoded to sRGB only when written to the screen; chapter 07 explains why that step matters.

Key idea. Final color = ambient + diffuse + specular, computed in linear space. Each term is a simple, separable knob, which is why this model ruled real-time graphics for decades.

STEP 5Where to evaluate: flat, Gouraud or per-pixel

We have a formula. The remaining question is how often to run it, and that is a pipeline question:

  • Flat shading: once per triangle, using the face normal. Cheapest; facets are obvious.
  • Gouraud shading (Henri Gouraud, 1971): once per vertex using vertex normals, in the vertex stage. The rasterizer then interpolates the resulting colors across the triangle.
  • Phong shading / per-pixel lighting (Phong, 1975): interpolate the normals instead, renormalize, and run the whole formula for every fragment. Not to be confused with the Phong reflection model of step 3; the same paper introduced both.

Gouraud's weakness is the highlight. A specular highlight is a small, sharp feature. If it lands in the middle of a big triangle, none of the three vertices sees it, so the interpolated color has no highlight at all. When the light moves, the highlight pops on as it crosses a vertex and blurs into a star shape along edges.

The heads-up display counts lighting evaluations. Flat runs the formula once per visible triangle, Gouraud once per vertex, per-pixel once per fragment: often 50 to 100 times more work. In 1990s hardware that cost was decisive. Today fragment shaders are cheap enough that per-pixel lighting is the default.

A short history

1971: Henri Gouraud publishes smooth shading by interpolating vertex intensities. 1975: Bui Tuong Phong publishes both his reflection model (ambient + diffuse + (R·V)ⁿ specular) and normal interpolation. 1977: Jim Blinn introduces the half-vector form (N·H)ⁿ.

This combination became the fixed-function lighting of OpenGL 1.x (glLight, glMaterial) and Direct3D's early versions: per-light ambient, diffuse and specular colors, Blinn-Phong specular, constant/linear/quadratic attenuation, all evaluated per vertex and then Gouraud-interpolated. Programmable shaders (chapter 05) moved lighting into the fragment shader, and physically based shading (chapter 07) replaced the ad-hoc terms.

Key idea. Where you evaluate lighting matters as much as the formula. Interpolating colors (Gouraud) loses features smaller than a triangle; interpolating normals and lighting per pixel keeps them.

RECAPWhat to remember

  • Normals describe orientation. Face normals give facets; interpolated vertex normals fake smooth curves. Transform normals with (M⁻¹)ᵀ and renormalize.
  • Lights: directional lights have one fixed L; point lights have a per-point L and attenuation 1/(k_c + k_l·d + k_q·d²).
  • Diffuse (Lambert): k_d · albedo · max(N·L, 0), independent of the viewer.
  • Specular: Phong (R·V)ⁿ or Blinn-Phong (N·H)ⁿ; higher shininess means a tighter highlight. Blinn-Phong needs about 4× the exponent for a similar size.
  • Ambient is a constant stand-in for bounced light. Final color = ambient + diffuse + specular, in linear space, encoded to sRGB for display.
  • Flat = per triangle, Gouraud = per vertex with interpolated colors (misses small highlights), Phong shading = per fragment with interpolated normals.
  • This is the classic fixed-function OpenGL lighting model (Gouraud 1971, Phong 1975, Blinn 1977). Chapter 07 replaces it with physically based shading.
