Coordinate Spaces & Transforms

Before a GPU can color a single pixel, every vertex has to make a journey: from the model's own coordinates, into the world, in front of a camera, through a projection, and finally onto a grid of pixels. This chapter follows one vertex all the way.

You'll learn

  • Why graphics uses 4D homogeneous coordinates, and what w is for.
  • Why matrix order matters (T·R ≠ R·T), and why MVP is written P·V·M.
  • How perspective and orthographic projection differ, and why depth precision is uneven.
  • How normalized device coordinates become pixel positions, including the y flip.

STEP 1Object space → world space

An artist builds a model around its own origin. A cube's corners might sit at (±0.5, ±0.5, ±0.5). That is object space (also called model space). It says nothing about where the cube is in the game. The model matrix M places the object in the shared world space: it scales it, rotates it and moves it.

Moving (translation) is the awkward part. A 3×3 matrix can scale and rotate, but it can't add a constant offset, because a matrix times the zero vector is always zero. The trick is to add a fourth coordinate, w. Points get w = 1, and a 4×4 matrix can then put the offset in its last column. These are homogeneous coordinates. As a bonus, directions such as normals get w = 0, so translation leaves them alone.

pworld=M pobjectM=T R S(scale first, then rotate, then translate)T(tx,ty,tz)=[100tx010ty001tz0001]T [x   y   z   1]T=[x+tx     y+ty     z+tz     1]Tpoint: movesT [x   y   z   0]T=[x   y   z   0]Tdirection: unchanged\begin{gathered} p_{\text{world}} = M\,p_{\text{object}} \qquad M = T\,R\,S \quad \text{(scale first, then rotate, then translate)}\\[10pt] T(t_x,t_y,t_z) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & t_x\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & t_y\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & t_z\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\\[10pt] \begin{aligned} T\,[x\;\,y\;\,z\;\,1]^{\mathsf T} &= [x+t_x\;\;\, y+t_y\;\;\, z+t_z\;\;\, 1]^{\mathsf T} && \text{point: moves}\\ T\,[x\;\,y\;\,z\;\,0]^{\mathsf T} &= [x\;\,y\;\,z\;\,0]^{\mathsf T} && \text{direction: unchanged} \end{aligned} \end{gathered}

With column vectors, the matrix closest to the point is applied first. So T·R·S·p means "scale, then rotate, then translate". Swap two of them and you get a different result. Try it below. With T·R the cube spins in place and then moves. With R·T it moves first, and the rotation then swings it around the world origin like a planet in orbit.

Key idea. A 4×4 matrix with w = 1 for points can scale, rotate and translate. Read a product of matrices right to left: the rightmost one acts on the vertex first.

STEP 2World space → view space

GPUs don't have a "camera" object. Instead, we move the whole world so that the camera sits at the origin and looks down −Z, with +Y up and +X to the right. The result is view space (eye or camera space). The view matrix V that does this is the inverse of the camera's own placement in the world. If the camera is 6 units in front of the cube, then in view space the cube is 6 units down −Z, at z = −6.

A common way to build V is lookAt(eye, target, up). It works out the camera's right vector r, up vector u and backward vector b (pointing away from the target), puts them in the rows, and subtracts the eye position:

V=[rxryrz−r⋅eyeuxuyuz−u⋅eyebxbybz−b⋅eye0001]b=normalize⁡(eye−target)r=normalize⁡(up×b)u=b×rV = \begin{bmatrix} r_x & r_y & r_z & -r\cdot\text{eye}\\ u_x & u_y & u_z & -u\cdot\text{eye}\\ b_x & b_y & b_z & -b\cdot\text{eye}\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \begin{aligned} b &= \operatorname{normalize}(\text{eye} - \text{target})\\ r &= \operatorname{normalize}(\text{up} \times b)\\ u &= b \times r \end{aligned}
Key idea. The view matrix doesn't move a camera. It moves the world into the camera's frame, so that "in front of the camera" always means "negative z".

STEP 3Projection: view space → clip space

The camera sees a region called the view frustum. For a perspective camera it's a pyramid with the tip cut off, bounded by a near plane, a far plane and the field of view (FOV). The projection matrix P reshapes the frustum into clip space. Here's the OpenGL perspective matrix, with n and f the near and far distances:

k=1tan⁡(fov/2)P=[kaspect0000k0000f+nn−f2fnn−f00−10]⇒wclip=−zview\begin{gathered} k = \frac{1}{\tan(\text{fov}/2)}\\[10pt] P = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{k}{\text{aspect}} & 0 & 0 & 0\\[6pt] 0 & k & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \dfrac{f+n}{n-f} & \dfrac{2fn}{n-f}\\[6pt] 0 & 0 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad w_{\text{clip}} = -z_{\text{view}} \end{gathered}

The bottom row is where perspective happens. It copies the vertex's distance in front of the camera, −z_view, into w. A point 8 units away gets w = 8, and one 2 units away gets w = 2. In the next step everything gets divided by w, so distant things shrink. An orthographic projection keeps w = 1. It just scales and shifts a box, so size no longer depends on distance. That suits CAD views, 2D games and shadow maps.

A point is inside the frustum exactly when −w ≤ x, y, z ≤ w. The GPU clips triangles against these planes in clip space, before any division, which is where the name comes from.

Key idea. The projection matrix doesn't divide anything. It puts depth into w (perspective) or leaves w = 1 (orthographic) and sets up the frustum so that "visible" means |x|, |y|, |z| ≤ w.

STEP 4Perspective divide → NDC, and why depth is nonlinear

After clipping, the GPU divides x, y and z by w. This is the perspective divide. It is fixed-function hardware, so your shader doesn't do it. The result is normalized device coordinates (NDC). The whole frustum becomes the cube [−1, 1]³, with the near plane at z = −1 and the far plane at z = +1 (OpenGL convention; chapter 08 covers APIs that use [0, 1]).

ndc=(xcw, ycw, zcw)zndc(d)=f+nf−n−2fn(f−n) dd=−zview=w(distance in front of the camera)\begin{gathered} \text{ndc} = \left(\frac{x_c}{w},\ \frac{y_c}{w},\ \frac{z_c}{w}\right)\\[10pt] z_{\text{ndc}}(d) = \frac{f+n}{f-n} - \frac{2fn}{(f-n)\,d} \qquad d = -z_{\text{view}} = w \quad \text{(distance in front of the camera)} \end{gathered}

Look at the depth formula. It is linear in 1/d, not in d. Most of the NDC depth range gets spent close to the near plane. With n = 0.1 and f = 100, half of all depth values cover only the first 0.2 units of the scene. Far away, objects that are centimetres apart can end up with the same stored depth. That causes z-fighting, where two surfaces flicker through each other. Pushing the near plane out helps far more than pulling the far plane in.

Key idea. Dividing by w is what makes distant things smaller. It also makes stored depth proportional to 1/distance, so precision is high near the camera and poor far away.

STEP 5Viewport: NDC → pixels

The last fixed step is the viewport transform. It stretches NDC x and y from [−1, 1] to the window size in pixels. There's one trap. NDC y points up, but this tutorial's screen space (like canvas, and most image formats) has its origin at the top-left with y pointing down. So y gets flipped. (OpenGL's own window coordinates actually have their origin at the bottom-left. The flip shown here is what you do when you draw into a top-left canvas.) Depth gets remapped too, from [−1, 1] to the depth buffer's [0, 1].

xpx=xndc+12 Wypx=1−yndc2 H←flip: NDC y up, screen y downdepth=zndc+12(default depth range 0…1)pixel (i,j) has its centre at (i+0.5, j+0.5)\begin{aligned} x_{\text{px}} &= \frac{x_{\text{ndc}} + 1}{2}\,W\\[4pt] y_{\text{px}} &= \frac{1 - y_{\text{ndc}}}{2}\,H && \leftarrow \text{flip: NDC } y \text{ up, screen } y \text{ down}\\[4pt] \text{depth} &= \frac{z_{\text{ndc}} + 1}{2} && \text{(default depth range } 0 \ldots 1\text{)}\\[8pt] & \text{pixel } (i, j) \text{ has its centre at } (i + 0.5,\ j + 0.5) \end{aligned}
Key idea. The viewport transform is a scale and an offset, plus a y flip when the screen's origin is at the top. The same NDC triangle lands on different pixels at every resolution.

STEP 6The whole chain, one vertex at a time

In a real vertex shader, the matrices are multiplied together once on the CPU, and each vertex does a single multiply: gl_Position = P · V · M · p. Read right to left: model, then view, then projection. The GPU does the rest (clip, divide, viewport). The table follows one corner of the cube through every stage. Key idea. Each space has one job. Object space is for authoring, world for placing, view for the camera, clip for clipping, NDC for a resolution-independent screen, and pixels for the framebuffer.

RECAPWhat to remember

  • Object → world: the model matrix M = T·R·S. Order matters, and the rightmost matrix acts first.
  • Homogeneous coordinates: points use w = 1 so 4×4 matrices can translate them. Directions use w = 0.
  • World → view: the view matrix moves the world so the camera is at the origin looking down −Z. Visible things have negative z.
  • View → clip: the projection matrix puts −z_view into w (perspective) or keeps w = 1 (orthographic). Visible means |x|, |y|, |z| ≤ w.
  • Perspective divide: xyz / w gives NDC in [−1, 1]³. Distant things shrink because they have a bigger w.
  • Depth is nonlinear: NDC z depends on 1/d. A near plane that is too small wastes precision and causes z-fighting far away.
  • Viewport: NDC is scaled to pixels, with y flipped for a top-left origin, and depth mapped to [0, 1].
  • MVP = P·V·M: the vertex shader outputs clip space. The GPU clips, divides and maps to the viewport. Next, chapter 02 turns those screen-space triangles into fragments.
