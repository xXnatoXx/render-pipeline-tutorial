Chapter 07 · Modern techniques

PBR, HDR & Post-Processing

Modern renderers try to obey physics: light is conserved, surfaces are made of tiny mirrors, and brightness is not capped at 1.0. This chapter follows a pixel from the physically based fragment shader, through a high-dynamic-range buffer, to the post-processing passes that turn it into the image on your screen.

STEP 1Surfaces are made of tiny mirrors

In chapter 03, the Blinn-Phong highlight was a handy trick: raise N·H to a "shininess" power and the spot gets smaller. It looks fine, but nothing stops it from reflecting more light than came in, and the shininess number has no physical meaning. Physically based rendering (PBR) replaces the trick with a model of what a surface really looks like up close.

Zoom in far enough on any surface and it stops being flat. Microfacet theory treats it as millions of tiny, perfectly flat mirrors, each tilted a little differently. A polished surface has facets that almost all point the same way, so light bounces off in one tight direction: a sharp reflection. A rough surface has facets pointing all over the place, so the same incoming light sprays across a wide cone: a blurry, spread-out highlight. Roughness is just "how much do the facet directions vary?"

The standard PBR specular model is the Cook-Torrance microfacet BRDF (Cook & Torrance, 1982). A BRDF answers one question: of the light arriving from direction l, how much leaves toward the eye v? Only facets whose normal equals the half vector h (halfway between l and v) can mirror light from l into v, so the formula counts those facets and corrects for a couple of effects:

f_spec(l, v) = D(h) · F(v, h) · G(l, v) ───────────────────── 4 (n·l) (n·v) D GGX / Trowbridge-Reitz: D(h) = α² / ( π · ((n·h)²(α² − 1) + 1)² ), α = roughness² F Schlick Fresnel: F = F0 + (1 − F0) · (1 − v·h)⁵ G Smith (Schlick-GGX form): G = G1(l) · G1(v), G1(x) = (n·x) / ((n·x)(1 − k) + k) k = (roughness + 1)² / 8 (Epic's choice for point lights)
  • D, the normal distribution, says what fraction of facets face along h. GGX (Walter et al. 2007; the same shape as Trowbridge-Reitz 1975) has a bright core and a long soft tail, which matches real materials better than older Gaussian-style lobes. Squaring roughness to get α makes the slider feel perceptually even.
  • F, Fresnel, says how much light a facet reflects instead of letting it in. Everything gets more mirror-like at grazing angles: look across a lake and it reflects the sky, look straight down and you see the bottom. F0 is the head-on reflectance. Non-metals ("dielectrics") sit near 0.04 (4%); metals reflect 50–100% and their F0 is colored, which is why gold reflections look gold.
  • G, geometry/shadowing-masking, removes light that is blocked by neighboring bumps on the way in (shadowing) or the way out (masking). It matters most on rough surfaces at grazing angles.
Key idea. Roughness is not a "blur" setting. It describes how spread out the microfacet normals are, and the GGX D term turns that statistic into the shape of the highlight. A rougher surface has a wider but dimmer lobe: the same energy, spread over more directions.

STEP 2Metallic and roughness: two sliders for every material

Artists don't want to type Fresnel coefficients. In 2012 Brent Burley's Disney course notes ("Physically-Based Shading at Disney") proposed a small set of intuitive, artist-friendly parameters, and in 2013 Brian Karis's notes "Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4" trimmed it down to what became the industry default: base color, metallic, roughness. Unity, Godot, glTF 2.0 and most other engines use essentially the same set.

The trick is that one base color serves two purposes. For a non-metal, the base color is the diffuse color (light that enters the surface, scatters, and comes back out tinted), and the specular reflectance is a fixed 4%. For a metal, there is no diffuse at all (metals absorb light that enters them), and the base color becomes the colored F0:

F0 = mix(0.04, baseColor, metallic) diffuse = (1 − F) · (1 − metallic) · baseColor / π color = (diffuse + f_spec) · lightRadiance · (n·l)

The (1 − F) factor is energy conservation in action: light that was reflected by the specular term is no longer available to enter the surface and become diffuse. The / π keeps a white Lambert surface from reflecting more light than it receives. Every sphere below is shaded per pixel with exactly these formulas.

That last observation is why PBR almost always comes with image-based lighting (IBL): the surroundings are stored in an environment map (often a cube map), and every direction in it acts as a light. To make this affordable, engines pre-blur the environment map into mip levels for increasing roughness, and store the rest of the BRDF integral in a small lookup table. Karis's 2013 notes call this the "split sum" approximation. The demo above uses a crude sky/ground gradient as a stand-in so you can see the effect.

Key idea. With only base color, metallic and roughness, the same shader produces plastic, paint, rubber, gold and steel, and every material stays plausible under any lighting, because the math conserves energy instead of relying on hand-tuned numbers.

STEP 3Linear light vs sRGB: do the math in the right space

Here is a subtle fact that trips up every graphics beginner: the number stored in an image file is not proportional to the amount of light. Images are usually stored in sRGB, which spends more of its 256 codes on dark tones, because our eyes are far more sensitive to differences in the shadows. Code 128 (0.5) is not half the light of code 255. It is only about 21% of it. Half the light is code 188 (about 0.735).

decode (sRGB → linear): c ≤ 0.04045 ? c / 12.92 : ((c + 0.055) / 1.055)^2.4 encode (linear → sRGB): c ≤ 0.0031308 ? 12.92 · c : 1.055 · c^(1/2.4) − 0.055 (close to a plain power curve: linear ≈ sRGB^2.2)

Light, on the other hand, adds and scales linearly: two equal lamps give twice the photons. So the rule is: decode textures to linear, do all lighting, blending and filtering in linear, and encode to sRGB only at the very end. GPUs help: an sRGB texture format decodes automatically when sampled, and an sRGB framebuffer encodes automatically when written.

Why is the left sphere wrong? Lambert says a surface tilted 60° from the light receives cos 60° = 0.5 of the light. Multiplying the sRGB value by 0.5 doesn't give half the light, it gives about a fifth of it. The whole falloff gets crushed toward black, and adding two lights or blending two textures goes wrong in the same way. Strip A shows why sRGB exists: its 16 steps look evenly spaced to your eye. Strip B is evenly spaced in physical light, and most of its steps look nearly white.

Key idea. sRGB is a storage encoding that matches human perception; linear is the working space where physics holds. Mixing them up is invisible in the code and very visible in the image: dark, muddy falloffs and blends that look wrong.

STEP 4HDR, exposure and tonemapping

Real scenes have enormous brightness ranges. The sun's disk can be many thousands of times brighter than a shaded wall, yet a monitor can only show values from 0 to 1. If the renderer clamps everything at 1.0 while it works, information is lost forever: a lamp at 20 and the sun at 50 both become plain white, and later effects like bloom can't tell them apart.

So modern pipelines render into an HDR render target, a floating-point texture (commonly a 16-bit-per-channel format such as RGBA16F, or the packed R11G11B10F) that happily stores 20.0 or 5000.0. Only at the end does a tonemapping pass squeeze that range into 0–1, like a camera:

exposed = sceneColor · 2^EV (exposure, in photographic stops) clamp = min(x, 1) Reinhard = x / (1 + x) (Reinhard et al. 2002, simple form) ACES fit = x(2.51x + 0.03) / (x(2.43x + 0.59) + 0.14) (Narkowicz's curve fit, clamped to [0, 1]) display = sRGB_encode( tonemap(exposed) )

Exposure picks which part of the range is "middle". Reinhard never quite reaches 1, so bright values roll off gently instead of clipping, but blacks look a little washed out. The ACES fit, a cheap approximation by Krzysztof Narkowicz of the film-like curve from the Academy Color Encoding System, adds a gentle "toe" for richer shadows and a "shoulder" for smooth highlights. Some versions scale the input by about 0.6 first; this page uses the plain fit.

Games usually adjust exposure automatically ("eye adaptation"): they measure the average log-luminance of the frame, often by downsampling it to a single pixel, and drift the exposure toward a target over a second or two, like your eyes adjusting when you walk out of a tunnel. Note that this demo tonemaps each RGB channel separately, which is common and cheap but can shift the hue of very saturated bright colors toward yellow or white.

Key idea. Keep light values physical (unclamped, linear) for as long as possible. Exposure and tonemapping are a final, deliberate "development" step, just like a photographer choosing how to print a negative.

STEP 5Bloom: letting bright light bleed

Real lenses and eyes aren't perfect. Very bright light scatters a little, so a street lamp at night appears wrapped in a soft glow. Bloom fakes this, and it only works well with HDR input: you want a lamp at 20.0 to glow far more than a white wall at 0.9, and in an 8-bit buffer both would just be 1.0.

A wide blur at full resolution would be far too slow, so engines build bloom from a chain of shrinking images. This structure was popularized by talks such as Jorge Jimenez's 2014 "Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare":

  1. Bright-pass: keep only the light above a threshold.
  2. Downsample chain: halve the resolution again and again (½, ¼, ⅛ …), blurring a little at each level. A small blur on a tiny image covers a huge area of the screen for almost no cost.
  3. Upsample and add: walk back up the chain, enlarging each level and adding it to the next larger one, so tight and wide glows combine.
  4. Composite: add the result to the HDR scene (scaled by an intensity), then tonemap.
Key idea. Bloom is a pyramid: cheap blurs on progressively smaller images, summed back up. Its strength depends on HDR, because only values above 1.0 should really glow.

STEP 6Anti-aliasing: four ways to fight jaggies

Chapter 02 tested each pixel's coverage with one sample at its center. An edge that cuts through a pixel is therefore either fully in or fully out, which gives staircase "jaggies" that crawl as things move. Every anti-aliasing method estimates how much of each pixel is really covered, but they spend their effort in very different places:

  • SSAA (supersampling): render at a higher resolution (here 4 samples per pixel) and average. Best quality, but it runs the fragment shader 4× as often.
  • MSAA (multisampling): test coverage and depth at 4 sample positions, but run the fragment shader once per pixel per triangle and copy the result to the covered samples. Edges get smooth for little shading cost, but it does nothing for aliasing inside a triangle (shiny highlights, alpha-tested foliage), and it fits awkwardly with deferred shading's G-buffer.
  • FXAA (Fast Approximate AA, Timothy Lottes, NVIDIA 2009): a post-process. Render normally, then find pixels with high luminance contrast, estimate the edge direction, and blend across it. Very cheap and works on anything, but it only sees the final image, so it can soften texture detail.
  • TAA (temporal AA): nudge (jitter) the camera by a different sub-pixel offset every frame and blend each new frame into an accumulated history buffer. Over 8–16 frames each pixel effectively collects many samples. To survive motion, the history is reprojected using per-pixel motion vectors and clamped to the colors around the current pixel. When that fails you get ghosting: smeared trails behind moving objects. Brian Karis's 2014 talk "High Quality Temporal Supersampling" is a classic description; upscalers like DLSS, FSR 2+ and XeSS build on the same idea.

In practice, many modern deferred renderers ship TAA (or a temporal upscaler) as the default, sometimes with a sharpening pass to counter its softness, while forward renderers, VR titles and mobile GPUs often still prefer MSAA, which tile-based mobile hardware can resolve very cheaply on chip.

Key idea. Anti-aliasing is about sampling. SSAA takes more samples in space, MSAA takes more coverage samples but not more shading, FXAA guesses from the finished image, and TAA spreads its samples across time and pays for it with blur and ghosting when motion confuses it.

RECAPWhat to remember

  • PBR models surfaces as microfacets. Cook-Torrance specular = D·F·G / (4 (n·l)(n·v)), with GGX for D, Schlick for F and Smith for G.
  • Roughness widens and dims the highlight. Fresnel makes everything more reflective at grazing angles. Dielectrics have F0 ≈ 0.04; metals have colored F0 and no diffuse.
  • Metallic/roughness (Disney 2012, Epic/UE4 2013): F0 = mix(0.04, base, metallic), and diffuse is scaled by (1 − F)(1 − metallic) so energy is conserved.
  • Environment lighting (IBL) is what makes metals look like metal; engines prefilter it per roughness level.
  • Store in sRGB, compute in linear. Decode textures, light in linear, encode once at the end.
  • Render into an HDR floating-point target, then apply exposure and a tonemapping curve (Reinhard, ACES-style filmic) to fit the display.
  • Bloom = bright-pass → downsample + blur chain → upsample + add → composite before tonemapping.
  • AA: SSAA (shade every sample, expensive), MSAA (coverage per sample, shading per pixel), FXAA (post-process edge blur), TAA (jittered samples accumulated over frames, watch for ghosting).
